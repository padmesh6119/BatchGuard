BatchGuard
Capacity-aware medicine risk ranking and live clone-scan early warning
IBM Z Datathon 2026 | Theme 1: Real-Time AI for Critical Decisions | Abstract submission | Team: [Team name]

One-line summary. BatchGuard tells a drug inspector which batch to sample first this week, and why. It
fuses a risk ranking learned from real CDSCO failure history with a live stream of verification-scan anomalies,
and returns an explained, ranked queue that fits the inspector's limited testing capacity. A human always
makes the final call.
The problem
Failed and counterfeit medicines are usually found after
harm has occurred. Regulators cannot test every batch,
and a copied verification code can make a fake pack look
genuine.

The solution
Two evidence layers, one decision: a risk prior from real
regulatory history and a live clone-suspicion detector,
fused into a capacity-aware inspection queue with reason
codes.

What makes it different
It optimises the scarce resource, inspector sampling
capacity. Success is measured as failures caught per 100
samples against simple baselines, not as a dashboard.

What is real and what is simulated
Layer A uses real CDSCO NSQ and spurious-drug lists.
Layer B is a simulated, labelled scan stream anchored to
real manufacturers and batches. Both are labelled
throughout.

1. The problem
• Coldrif, September to October 2025. At least 22 children (some reports say 24) died in Madhya Pradesh
and Rajasthan after taking a cough syrup reported to contain 48.6 percent diethylene glycol against a 0.1
percent limit.

• Slow detection. Deaths began in September and CDSCO reported the contamination to WHO on 8 October. A
central team took 19 samples from the children's doctors and nearby shops; only 4 were declared Not of
Standard Quality (NSQ).

• Nobody can test everything. India has over 10,000 registered drug manufacturers, and enforcement falls
largely on state drug controllers with limited capacity. The real question is what to test first.

• The data exists, and so do counterfeits. CDSCO publishes NSQ and spurious-drug lists every month (trade
press reported 157 NSQ and 2 spurious samples for May 2026). A spurious drug is made by an unauthorised
entity and sold under a legitimate brand.
The gap. Published lists arrive after the fact and cover only tested samples. A static code can be copied onto
any number of fake packs. Inspectors have no principled, evidence-based way to decide what to sample first.
The people harmed are patients (especially children) and honest manufacturers whose names get attached to
fakes.

2. Our solution
BatchGuard answers one operational question: which batch should an inspector sample first this week,
and why? The output is an order, not a verdict, because testing capacity is fixed. Each layer covers the other's
blind spot.

4. Layer A: the real-data core
• Data. CDSCO monthly NSQ and spurious-drug lists: product, batch number, manufacturing and expiry dates,
manufacturer, failure reason, reporting source and month. Fields and at least 12 usable months are confirmed
in the first two hours of the build.

• Features. Manufacturer failure history (counts, recency), product category and dosage form (for example oral
liquids against injectables), failure type, state, batch age at test, and a missing-state flag. Rates are used
wherever a month publishes the number of samples tested.

• Model and split. Snap ML ranking model trained on lists to August 2025 and tested on September 2025 to
August 2026, a chronological split with no leakage across time.

• Baselines. Random selection and "most past failures". A predictive claim is made only if BatchGuard beats the
stronger of the two on held-out months.

• First-time failures. A manufacturer with no earlier failure is invisible to history alone. Layer A carries
product-form risk as a coarse prior for such cases, and the Coldrif backtest reports plainly what history would
and would not have shown. The live layer exists for exactly this gap.

• Sampling bias. Only tested batches appear, and some states submit nothing in some months. The queue
shows denominators and flags missing states instead of hiding them.

5. Layer B: live clone-suspicion detection
• Simulated, labelled stream. Verification scans seeded with real manufacturer and batch identities. No
simulated result is presented as real-world counterfeit-detection performance.

• Benign behaviour is simulated too. Repeat scans by the same buyer, offline sync delays, pharmacist bulk
checks and legitimate travel, so an unusual scan is not automatically treated as suspicious.

• Attack patterns. Code copied across distant locations, bursts of scans on one identity, a slow adaptive
attacker who stays under rate thresholds, and scan flooding to create false alerts. At least one attack type is
held out from the detector's design and used only for testing.

• Detector. Per-identity features (scan velocity, geographic spread, source diversity, time pattern) feed a rule
baseline and an ML detector. Every alert carries a reason code.

• Flood resistance. Rate limits, independent-source counts and required corroboration: many scans from one
source count for little, a few scans from many independent sources count for more.

6. Fusion and the human decision
Each queue item shows three separate values, not one opaque score: historical risk, scan anomaly and
evidence quality (how many independent sources, how recent, how consistent). The combined priority is a
triage aid and always lists its reason codes, for example clone pattern, manufacturer history, no sample yet.
Capacity-aware ranking. The inspector sets how many samples can be drawn this week. BatchGuard returns
the best list of that size, and the dashboard shows how much expected coverage each extra sample would add.

7. Role of IBM LinuxONE
• Verification scans run as a continuous event stream through a scoring service on LinuxONE; Snap ML is used for
training and scoring.

• The same defined workload is run on LinuxONE and on a laptop. We record event count, duration, sustained
events per second, p95 and p99 latency, and software versions.

• We report only what actually ran on LinuxONE. Platform security features are mentioned only if configured and
tested, and no speed advantage is claimed beyond what the measurements show.

9. Safety, privacy and honest limits
• Privacy. Scan identities are pseudonymised and location is coarsened to district level. No personal profile of
buyers is built.

• Triage, not proof. Scores are triage estimates, not proof of safety, quality failure or counterfeiting. The
dashboard states this.

• Sampling bias. Published lists reflect tested samples, not all batches. Coverage gaps are shown, not hidden.
• Simulated scan layer. It tests system behaviour under stated conditions and does not establish real-world
detection performance.

• Benign anomalies. Repeat or distant scans can be legitimate; corroboration, rate limits and human review
handle them.

10. Impact and path to a pilot
• Who acts. State drug controllers and CDSCO inspectors, through a ranked sampling queue, on Monday
morning.

• Who benefits. Patients, especially children who take liquid medicines; honest manufacturers whose names
are attached to fakes; and inspectors with limited capacity.

• Pilot path. Run the risk layer on published monthly lists for one state, with inspectors comparing the queue
against their usual sampling plan. The scan layer would plug into real verification traffic only with regulator and
manufacturer agreement.
